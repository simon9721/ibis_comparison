Part 1: The Physical Reality Inside a Real CMOS Output Buffer
A CMOS output buffer is not a simple inverter. The actual circuit looks like this:

    ┌─────[Pre-driver A (NAND/NOR)]─────► GUP ──► PMOS gate
inp_pin ────────────┤
                    └─────[Pre-driver B (NAND/NOR)]─────► GDN ──► NMOS gate
                                                                      │
                                                            PMOS drain ┤ V_out
                                                            NMOS drain ┘
This is not a coincidence of design — it is a deliberate architectural choice. A pullup driver transistor is coupled to an output pad for driving the output pad to higher voltages, the pullup driver transistor being controlled by a first driver-gate node; a pulldown driver transistor is coupled to the output pad for driving the output pad to lower voltages, the pulldown driver transistor being controlled by a second driver-gate node; a first pre-driver for driving the first driver-gate node; a second pre-driver for driving the second driver-gate node.
uspto

Why two separate pre-drivers? Because the PMOS and NMOS gate drives deliberately have different timing: the first predriver is designed to have the first NOT gate having a turn-on time different from a turn-on time of the second NOT gate to reduce a short circuit current of the steady-state driver.
Wordpress

And this creates explicitly asymmetric gate drive: the first drive signal is connected to the gate of the output stage's PMOS transistor, and the second drive signal is connected to the gate of the output stage's NMOS transistor. Each output device turns on relatively slowly, but turns off relatively fast.

Ku(t) and Kd(t) are not abstract mathematical coefficients — they are behavioral proxies for the voltage at GUP and GDN respectively. Specifically:

Ku(t) → how much PMOS is conducting → determined by V_GUP at that instant
Kd(t) → how much NMOS is conducting → determined by V_GDN at that instant
When you extract Ku(t) and Kd(t) from V-T waveforms, what you are really recovering is the time history of V_GUP and V_GDN during a complete characterized transition. The 1.65 ns gap you measured between TF_KU and TF_KD is simply the time separation between GUP reaching its target and GDN reaching its target — caused exactly by the separate pre-driver timing described above.

Part 3: What the Two-State Model Does
Instead of storing Ku and Kd as time-indexed tables and replaying them, the model stores the current state of GUP and GDN as continuous voltage variables, each evolving with its own dynamics:

         ─► [Pre-driver B RC] ─► GDN  →  Kd = f_pd(GDN)  →  NMOS current

Part 4: Why Mid-Transition Reversal Is Handled Correctly
This is the key part you wanted to understand clearly.

At the moment of reversal (t = t_rev), GUP and GDN each hold their current voltage. In ngspice, C_gup and C_gdn store charge — they physically cannot jump. When the input reverses:

Before reversal:
  GUP = 0.6 V  (PMOS partially turned off, Ku = 0.6)
  GDN = 0.8 V  (NMOS partially turned on, Kd = 0.8)

At t_rev, input reverses:
  gup_tgt flips → GUP now targets its ON value
  gdn_tgt flips → GDN now targets its OFF value

After reversal:
  GUP evolves from 0.6 toward ON at rate τ_ku_on
  GDN evolves from 0.8 toward OFF at rate τ_kd_off
  Each independently, at its own rate

  Ku = f_pu(GUP) follows GUP continuously
  Kd = f_pd(GDN) follows GDN continuously
No table switch. No reset. No trajectory mismatch. GUP and GDN are just voltages on capacitors evolving toward new targets — exactly as the physical pre-driver RC charges and discharges in the real silicon.

Part 5: Why This Is Physically Correct — The Argument
The claim of physical correctness rests on three steps:

Step 1: In the real silicon, PMOS current at any instant is determined solely by V_GUP (and V_out). NMOS current is determined solely by V_GDN (and V_out). This is basic MOSFET physics — the transistor has no memory of how it got to its current gate voltage.

Step 2: GUP and GDN are separate physical nodes with separate RC dynamics (separate pre-driver paths, separate gate capacitances). Their time evolution is independent, governed by first-order RC equations in the lumped approximation.

Step 3: When the input reverses, GUP and GDN each respond according to their own RC dynamics, starting from their current voltage. The charge stored on their gate capacitances is the only state that matters. This is what the two-state model implements.

The model is a behavioral approximation of this, not a perfect replica — the RC is a lumped approximation to the distributed pre-driver chain, and f_pu/f_pd are 1D mappings rather than full 2D MOSFET surfaces. But the state continuity and independence are captured correctly, which is what the replay and single-RC approaches both fail to do.

Step 1 — τ values:

From your Ku(t) table for a complete rising transition, fit an exponential to the slope segment:

Ku(t) ≈ Ku_final + (Ku_initial - Ku_final) · e^(-(t - t_onset)/τ_ku_off)
Do the same for the falling transition → τ_ku_on. Repeat for Kd → τ_kd_on, τ_kd_off. You get four numbers.

Step 2 — f_pu and f_pd mappings:

V_GUP(t) = 1 - e^(-(t - t_onset)/τ_ku_off)   (normalized 0→1)
You also know Ku(t) from your table. So at each time point you can form the pair (V_GUP(t), Ku(t)). Plot those pairs — that is f_pu. It is a PWL curve from GUP → Ku, which you put into the B_ku behavioral source.

Step 3 — Validation:

Now — Why This Is Correct (#3)
Here's the crux. Look at what happened at the reversal point and ask: what would the replay approach have done instead?

ACTUAL physical state at reversal:
  GUP = 0.368, GDN = 0.465
  (PMOS partly off, NMOS partly on — a specific mid-transition condition)

REPLAY approach at reversal:
  Input crosses 0.6V → hard switch to "FALLING" Ku/Kd tables
  Those tables start from GUP=1.0, GDN=0.0 (fully settled HIGH state)
  → it assumes the buffer was fully settled when it wasn't
  → wrong starting point → wrong current from here on
The two-state model is correct because of three linked facts:

Fact 1 — The transistor current depends only on its present gate state, not its history.
A real NMOS conducting at GDN=0.465 produces a specific current regardless of whether it was on its way up or down. Kd = f_pd(0.465) gives that current correctly. The model never needs to know "which transition we're in" — only the current value of GDN. This is just MOSFET physics.

Fact 2 — Gate charge cannot teleport.
In real silicon, GUP and GDN are voltages on the PMOS and NMOS gate capacitances. When the input reverses, those gates discharge/charge from their present voltage — they physically cannot jump to a "settled" value. The model stores GUP and GDN as voltages on capacitors (C_gup, C_gdn), so they also can't jump. The model's state continuity mirrors the silicon's charge continuity.

Fact 3 — PMOS and NMOS are driven independently.
The 1.65 ns gap in your data is the physical signature of separate pre-driver paths with different timing. The model gives GUP and GDN their own independent τ values, so it reproduces that gap. The single-RC model forced GUP = 1 − GDN, which is physically false for a buffer with separate pre-drivers.

The One-Sentence Version
The model is correct because it tracks the two things that actually have physical state in the buffer — the PMOS gate voltage and the NMOS gate voltage — lets each evolve at its own real rate, and computes current from their instantaneous values. The replay approach fails because it has no state; it just re-reads a pre-recorded trajectory that assumes the buffer always starts from a settled corner, which is exactly false during a runt pulse.

Kd
1.0 |              ........———————
    |          .                      ← slow approach to 1 (saturation)
    |        .
0.5 |      .                          ← fast in the middle
    |    .

4 segments → 4 numbers:  τ_kd_on, τ_kd_off, τ_ku_on, τ_ku_off
For short-pulse SI work this is often the right choice — the runt pulse spends most of its time in the early/middle region, and the exact shape of the saturation tail matters less. Simplest to implement, four B-source rate equations.

  GDN < 0.2:   τ = 0.62 ns   (slow start)
  GDN 0.2–0.8: τ = 0.40 ns   (fast middle)
  GDN > 0.8:   τ = 0.55 ns   (slow tail)

spice

This is more accurate but more complex.

The cleaner way to think about Option 2
If you find yourself building a τ-vs-state table, there's a mathematically equivalent and simpler formulation: drop the exponential model entirely and go back to mapping state directly to coefficient via f_pu/f_pd.

Here's why they're equivalent. Both the "τ table" and "f mapping" are just two ways of encoding the same curve shape:

τ(state) table:  encodes the SLOPE of the curve at each state
f(state) mapping: encodes the VALUE of the curve at each state
If your curve isn't a clean exponential, it's usually cleaner to store the value directly (f mapping) than to store slopes (τ table) and integrate them. So the practical hierarchy is:

Curve is clean exponential     → 1 number per segment        (4 numbers)
Curve is mildly non-exponential → 1 number, accept small error (4 numbers)
Curve is strongly S-shaped      → store f(state) mapping directly (the PWL approach)
You rarely want the middle ground of a τ-table; you either accept one number or go to the full mapping.

The τ-number approach and the f-mapping approach are the two clean endpoints; the τ-table is an awkward middle you generally skip.

Good — this is the piece that ties it all together. Let me define it precisely, because it's doing something subtly different from τ.

The Two Different Jobs
There are two separate questions in the model, and they need two separate pieces of information:

Question 1: "How fast does the gate node move?"        → answered by τ
Question 2: "Given the gate is at state X, how much
             does the transistor actually conduct?"     → answered by f_pu / f_pd
f_pu and f_pd answer Question 2. They are the map from gate-drive state to conduction coefficient.

Precise Definition
GUP = the state variable (0 to 1) representing how far the PMOS gate has moved
f_pu(GUP) = Ku = the fraction of full PMOS current that flows at that gate state

GDN = the state variable (0 to 1) representing how far the NMOS gate has moved
f_pd(GDN) = Kd = the fraction of full NMOS current that flows at that gate state
So in the current equation:

I_pullup  = f_pu(GUP) × I_pu(V_die)     ← Ku × static pullup current
I_pulldown = f_pd(GDN) × I_pd(V_die)    ← Kd × static pulldown current
f_pu converts "where the gate is" into "how strongly the transistor drives."

Why It's Not Just GUP = Ku Directly
Here's the subtlety. The gate voltage rising linearly does not make the transistor current rise linearly. The MOSFET has a threshold and a nonlinear transfer characteristic:

Gate voltage rising 0 → 1:        Transistor conduction:

V_gate                            Conduction (Ku)
1.0 |        ___                  1.0 |          ___———
    |      /                          |        /
    |    /                            |       |          ← steep once past threshold
0.5 |  /                          0.5 |      |
    | /                               |     |
0.0 |/____________ t              0.0 |_____/________ gate state
                                            ↑
                                       nothing until
                                       threshold crossed
The gate can be 30% of the way up and the transistor still conducts almost nothing (below threshold). Then once past threshold, conduction shoots up. f_pu encodes this nonlinear gate-to-conduction relationship.

Part 1: Confirming Your Understanding of f_pu
"f_pu is just the mapping of current voltage to a Ku? like Ku(Vgate)/Ku(VGUP)?"

Yes — exactly. f_pu is a function whose input is the gate state voltage GUP and whose output is Ku:

Ku = f_pu(GUP)
You could absolutely write it as Ku(VGUP). It's the same thing. It answers: "if the PMOS gate node is currently at this voltage/state, what fraction of full conduction is happening?" Stateless, just a lookup of the present gate voltage. That's correct.

Part 2: Deriving GUP(t) = 1 − e^(−t/τ)
This comes from the physical fact that the gate node is a capacitor being charged through a resistance. Let me build it from scratch.

The physical picture
The PMOS gate (node GUP) is driven by the pre-driver. The pre-driver has some output resistance R, and the gate itself has capacitance C. So the gate node is just an RC circuit:

    R
  V_target ──/\/\/──┬──── GUP (the gate node)
                    │
                   ─┴─ C   (gate capacitance)
                    │
                   GND
When the input flips, the pre-driver tries to drive GUP to a new target voltage V_target (either 0 or 1 in normalized terms). But it can't get there instantly — it has to charge C through R.

The differential equation
The current charging the capacitor is the voltage across R divided by R:

I_charge = (V_target − GUP) / R
That same current builds charge on the capacitor:

I_charge = C · dGUP/dt
Setting them equal:

C · dGUP/dt = (V_target − GUP) / R
Rearranging:

dGUP/dt = (V_target − GUP) / (R·C)
Define τ = R·C (the time constant). This is the rate equation you saw in the ngspice B-source:

dGUP/dt = (V_target − GUP) / τ
Solving it
This is a first-order linear ODE. For a constant target, the solution is a decaying exponential approach. Starting from GUP(0) = GUP_start and heading toward V_target:

GUP(t) = V_target + (GUP_start − V_target) · e^(−t/τ)
Plugging in the turn-ON case
For turning the PMOS ON from fully off: GUP_start = 0, V_target = 1:

GUP(t) = 1 + (0 − 1) · e^(−t/τ)
       = 1 − e^(−t/τ)
There it is. The 1 is the target, and the −e^(−t/τ) is the lagging approach as the capacitor charges.

Plugging in the turn-OFF case
For turning OFF from fully on: GUP_start = 1, V_target = 0:

GUP(t) = 0 + (1 − 0) · e^(−t/τ)
       = e^(−t/τ)
A decay from 1 down to 0. (This is the form I used in the worked example's Phase 1 for GUP heading off.)

The general reversal case (why it handles short pulses)
The general solution works from any starting value, which is the whole point. If the input reverses when GUP is at 0.368 (as in the worked example), and the new target is 1:

GUP(t') = 1 + (0.368 − 1) · e^(−t'/τ)
        = 1 − 0.632 · e^(−t'/τ)
GUP_start is just whatever the state currently is. The equation doesn't care how it got there — it picks up from the present value and heads to the new target. That continuity is exactly why the runt-pulse reversal is handled correctly.

Part 3: How GUP(t) and f_pu Work Together
Two separate things, and it's worth being crisp about which is which:

GUP(t) = 1 − e^(−t/τ)     ← TIME behavior: how the gate node moves through time

What a PWL τ Would Do

Here's the trap. You now have two places encoding the curve's shape:

τ(V_GUP)     → encodes how fast the gate moves at each state  (shape info #1)
f_pu(V_GUP)  → encodes conduction at each state                (shape info #2)
The problem: the same physical nonlinearity gets split across both, and they can double-count or fight each other. The S-shape of Ku(t) comes from two sources mixed together:

Source 1: the gate trajectory isn't a clean exponential (RC nonlinearity)
Source 2: conduction vs. gate voltage is nonlinear (MOSFET transfer curve)
When you fit a single τ + f_pu, there's a clean division of labor: τ owns the timing, f_pu owns the conduction nonlinearity. When you make τ a PWL function of state, you're now letting τ also absorb shape — and there's no unique way to decide how much shape goes into τ(V_GUP) versus f_pu(V_GUP). τ should represent R_predriver × C_gate — a property of the CIRCUIT
R and C are roughly constant for a given pre-driver. You'd be encoding transfer-curve nonlinearity as if it were timing nonlinearity. That's physically wrong, and it's wrong in a way that only shows up under reversal.

The Correct Hierarchy (restated with PWL τ placed properly)
Tier 1:  single τ + f_pu = identity
         (assumes Ku(t) is a clean exponential)

         τ owns timing, f_pu owns ALL the conduction nonlinearity

         shape split ambiguously across both
         better complete-transition fit, but
         ill-defined reversal behavior

# From Ku(t)/Kd(t) to a Gate-Voltage Buffer Model — Complete Walkthrough

A step-by-step work-through of the proposed short-pulse IBIS buffer model, starting
from already-extracted switching coefficients and ending at a running ngspice
simulation. Every stage uses a concrete mock example so the numbers can be followed
end to end.

---

## 0. The Core Idea in One Paragraph

The original model replays `Ku(t)`/`Kd(t)` as functions of *time since the edge*.
That breaks for short pulses, because when the input reverses mid-transition the
time index resets and the model replays a trajectory that assumes the buffer started
from a fully settled state. The new model removes time as the driving variable.
Instead it reconstructs the **gate voltage** of each output transistor as a continuous
state variable, and expresses conduction as a function of that gate voltage. Time
never indexes anything; the gate voltage simply evolves continuously and conduction
is read off it at every instant.

Three quantities replace the time-indexed tables:

| Quantity            | Meaning                              | Where it comes from                                 |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| `target`          | where the gate*should* go (on/off) | live comparison of input voltage                    |
| `tau`             | how fast the gate moves              | fit from the Ku/Kd curve timing                     |
| `f_pu` / `f_pd` | conduction at a given gate voltage   | the Ku/Kd curve shape, re-expressed vs gate voltage |

---

## 2. Reminder — Where "Gate Voltage" Comes From

There is no gate-voltage table in IBIS. The gate node is *inside* the buffer and the
behavioral model deliberately hides it. We reconstruct it:

```
V-T waveform  --(converter, already done)-->  Ku(t), Kd(t)
Ku(t), Kd(t)  --(fit exponential)--------->  tau   (the gate-node RC)
tau           --(integrate during sim)----->  GUP(t)  (reconstructed gate voltage)
```

`GUP` is a normalized state: `1.0` = PMOS gate fully driven to the conducting rail,
`0.0` = gate fully driven to the off rail. It is a voltage on a model capacitor that
the solver integrates — never read from IBIS, never read from the output node.

---

## 3. Step A — Fit tau (the timing)

Take one curve and fit it to a first-order exponential. For `Ku_fall` (PMOS turning
on), the canonical form for an on-transition is:

```
Ku(t) = 1 - e^(-(t - t_onset)/tau_ku_on)
```

### Identify the onset delay

`Ku_fall` is already moving at t=0.3 (Ku=0.08), so the dead time is small; take
`t_onset ≈ 0` for this mock.

### Linearize and fit

Rearrange: `ln(1 - Ku) = -(t)/tau`. Compute `ln(1 - Ku)` at each point:

| t (ns) | Ku   | 1 − Ku | ln(1 − Ku) | implied tau = −t / ln(1−Ku) |
| ------ | ---- | ------- | ----------- | ----------------------------- |
| 0.3    | 0.08 | 0.92    | −0.083     | 3.61                          |
| 0.6    | 0.30 | 0.70    | −0.357     | 1.68                          |
| 0.9    | 0.62 | 0.38    | −0.968     | 0.93                          |
| 1.2    | 0.85 | 0.15    | −1.897     | 0.63                          |
| 1.5    | 0.96 | 0.04    | −3.219     | 0.47                          |

The implied tau is **not constant** (3.61 down to 0.47). That tells us the curve is
not a clean single exponential — it is S-shaped. A least-squares fit through the
middle gives a representative value; weighting the central region (where most of the
transition happens) yields approximately:

```
tau_ku_on ≈ 0.6 ns
```

Repeat for `Ku_rise` (PMOS off-transition, form `Ku(t) = e^(-t/tau_ku_off)`):

| t (ns) | Ku   | ln(Ku)  | implied tau = −t / ln(Ku) |
| ------ | ---- | ------- | -------------------------- |
| 0.2    | 0.95 | −0.051 | 3.90                       |
| 0.4    | 0.78 | −0.248 | 1.61                       |
| 0.6    | 0.50 | −0.693 | 0.87                       |
| 0.8    | 0.22 | −1.514 | 0.53                       |
| 1.0    | 0.05 | −2.996 | 0.33                       |

Representative fit:

```
tau_ku_off ≈ 0.5 ns
```

So PMOS turns off slightly faster than it turns on — a real asymmetry the single-RC
model could not represent. The NMOS fits (not shown) give `tau_kd_on` and
`tau_kd_off`, which in a real buffer differ substantially from the PMOS values; that
difference is the physical origin of the TF_KU vs TF_KD gap.

**Output of Step A:** four numbers — `tau_ku_on`, `tau_ku_off`, `tau_kd_on`,
`tau_kd_off`.

## 5. Step C — Assemble the Simulation Model

Three live pieces per transistor.

### Target (direction) — set by the input, instantaneously

```
input HIGH (> V_th):  PMOS off -> GUP_target = 0 ;  NMOS on  -> GDN_target = 1
input LOW  (< V_th):  PMOS on  -> GUP_target = 1 ;  NMOS off -> GDN_target = 0
```

### Gate state evolution (rate) — RC toward target, direction-dependent tau

```
dGUP/dt = (GUP_target - GUP) / tau_ku   where tau_ku = tau_ku_on if rising else tau_ku_off
dGDN/dt = (GDN_target - GDN) / tau_kd   where tau_kd = tau_kd_on if rising else tau_kd_off
```

### Conduction and output current

```
Ku = f_pu(GUP)
Kd = f_pd(GDN)
I_out = Ku * I_pu(V_die) + Kd * I_pd(V_die) + I_clamps(V_die)
C_comp * dV_die/dt = I_out
```

---

## 6. Step D — Worked Short-Pulse Simulation

Now run a runt pulse and watch the state stay continuous through the reversal.

### Setup

```
VDD = 1.2 V, V_th = 0.6 V
tau_ku_on = 0.6 ns, tau_ku_off = 0.5 ns
tau_kd_on = 0.8 ns, tau_kd_off = 0.4 ns   (NMOS slower on, faster off)
Initial (output HIGH, input LOW): GUP = 1.0, GDN = 0.0
```

### The pulse: input goes HIGH at t=0, back LOW at t=0.5 ns (a 0.5 ns runt)

### Phase 1 — input HIGH (0 to 0.5 ns)

Targets flip: GUP_target = 0 (PMOS off), GDN_target = 1 (NMOS on).

```
GUP decays from 1.0 toward 0 at tau_ku_off = 0.5 ns:
  GUP(t) = e^(-t/0.5)
  GUP(0.5) = e^(-1.0) = 0.368

GDN rises from 0.0 toward 1 at tau_kd_on = 0.8 ns:
  GDN(t) = 1 - e^(-t/0.8)
  GDN(0.5) = 1 - e^(-0.625) = 0.465
```

At the reversal instant:

```
GUP = 0.368   (PMOS partly turned off)
GDN = 0.465   (NMOS only partly turned on -- slow, and pulse was short)
```

Note `GUP + GDN = 0.833`, not 1. They are independent. Conduction at this instant:

```
Ku = f_pu(0.368) ~ 0.30
Kd = f_pd(0.465) ~ 0.40   (using each transistor's own map)
```

The output has been pulled down only weakly and briefly — a partial dip, not a full
swing. This is the runt pulse.

### Phase 2 — input back LOW (from 0.5 ns)

Targets flip back: GUP_target = 1, GDN_target = 0. **Each state continues from its
current value** — no reset.

```
GUP rises from 0.368 toward 1 at tau_ku_on = 0.6 ns:
  GUP(t') = 1 - (1 - 0.368) * e^(-t'/0.6) = 1 - 0.632 * e^(-t'/0.6)

GDN decays from 0.465 toward 0 at tau_kd_off = 0.4 ns:
  GDN(t') = 0.465 * e^(-t'/0.4)
```

where t' = t − 0.5 ns. Tabulating the recovery:

| t' (ns) | GUP   | GDN   | Ku=f_pu(GUP) | Kd=f_pd(GDN) |
| ------- | ----- | ----- | ------------ | ------------ |
| 0.0     | 0.368 | 0.465 | 0.30         | 0.40         |
| 0.1     | 0.485 | 0.362 | 0.42         | 0.31         |
| 0.2     | 0.547 | 0.282 | 0.49         | 0.24         |
| 0.4     | 0.713 | 0.171 | 0.67         | 0.14         |
| 0.8     | 0.857 | 0.058 | 0.80         | 0.05         |
| 1.2     | 0.928 | 0.020 | 0.91         | 0.02         |

The PMOS recovers (Ku climbing back to ~1), the NMOS shuts off quickly (Kd → 0), and
the output charges back to VDD. The whole event is a small, brief dip that recovers —
exactly the physically correct runt-pulse response.

### Why this is correct, in one line

At the reversal the model used the *actual* gate states (0.368, 0.465). The replay
model would have hard-switched to the "falling" tables, which assume the buffer
started fully settled (GUP=1, GDN=0) — a state the buffer was never in. The new model
never resets because gate charge cannot jump; it just heads to a new target from
where it is.

## 7. Step E — ngspice Implementation

```spice
.subckt ibis_buf  inp  die  vdd  gnd  PARAMS:
+ Vth=0.6 c_comp=2p
+ tau_ku_on=0.6n tau_ku_off=0.5n tau_kd_on=0.8n tau_kd_off=0.4n

* ---- targets (direction), set live from the input ----
B_gup_t  gup_t 0  V = (V(inp) > Vth) ? 0 : 1
B_gdn_t  gdn_t 0  V = (V(inp) > Vth) ? 1 : 0

* ---- gate state GUP: capacitor integrates dGUP/dt = (target-GUP)/tau ----
C_gup  gup 0  1   IC=1
B_gup  gup 0  I = (V(gup_t) - V(gup)) /
+   (V(gup_t) > V(gup) ? tau_ku_on : tau_ku_off)

* ---- gate state GDN ----
C_gdn  gdn 0  1   IC=0
B_gdn  gdn 0  I = (V(gdn_t) - V(gdn)) /
+   (V(gdn_t) > V(gdn) ? tau_kd_on : tau_kd_off)

* ---- conduction maps f_pu(GUP), f_pd(GDN) ----
B_ku  ku 0  V = pwl(V(gup), 0,0, 0.2,0.10, 0.4,0.35, 0.6,0.55, 0.8,0.80, 1.0,1.0)
B_kd  kd 0  V = pwl(V(gdn), 0,0, 0.2,0.12, 0.4,0.34, 0.6,0.58, 0.8,0.82, 1.0,1.0)

* ---- driver currents: Ku * I_pu(V_die) + Kd * I_pd(V_die) ----
* I_pu VCC-referenced, I_pd GND-referenced (mock PWL tables)
B_pu  vdd die  I = V(ku) * pwl(V(vdd)-V(die), -1.2,-0.090, 0,0, 0.6,0.027, 1.2,0.030, 2.4,0.032)
B_pd  die gnd  I = V(kd) * pwl(V(die),         -1.2,-0.090, 0,0, 0.6,0.027, 1.2,0.030, 2.4,0.032)

* ---- clamps (always on, no Ku/Kd) ----
B_gc  die gnd  I = pwl(V(die),        -1.2,-0.050, 0,0, 1.2,0)
B_pc  vdd die  I = pwl(V(vdd)-V(die), -1.2,-0.050, 0,0, 1.2,0)

* ---- output capacitance ----
C_comp die 0  c_comp
.ends ibis_buf
```

Key points:

- `C_gup`/`C_gdn` are unit capacitors whose voltage *is* the gate state; the
  `B_gup`/`B_gdn` current sources implement `dGUP/dt = (target − GUP)/tau`.
- The `?:` on tau selects on-rate vs off-rate by the direction of travel.
- Nothing is indexed by time. The solver supplies time through normal transient
  integration; tau is a fixed circuit parameter, not a clock.
- Set `.options DELMAX` small enough (e.g. ~1/10 of the shortest pulse) so the runt
  dip is not stepped over.

---

## 8. Step F — Validation Before Trusting Short Pulses

1. **Consistency (long pulse):** drive a complete transition into the IBIS V-T load
   condition. The new model output must match the IBIS V-T waveform within the FOM
   threshold (typically >=95% curve-area). This proves tau and f_pu were extracted
   faithfully. On a complete transition the new model and the old replay model should
   agree.
2. **Payoff (short pulse):** drive a runt pulse. Here the new model should *diverge*
   from the replay model and produce the physically continuous partial-dip response.
   If it agreed with replay here, the new model would add nothing.
3. **Single-map check:** confirm the (GUP, Ku) clouds from the rise and fall curves
   overlap within tolerance. Good overlap validates the single-`f_pu` assumption.
   Poor overlap means refine tau or enrich `f_pu` (never make tau state-dependent).

---

## 9. Summary Pipeline

```
Ku(t), Kd(t)              (already extracted by converter)
   |
   |-- fit exponential ----> tau_ku_on/off, tau_kd_on/off        [Step A]
   |
   |-- reconstruct GUP(t), pair with Ku, drop time
   |       -> f_pu(GUP), f_pd(GDN)                                [Step B]
   |
   v
SIMULATION MODEL                                                  [Step C]
   target  = threshold(input)            (direction)
   dGate/dt = (target - Gate)/tau        (rate, RC integration)
   K        = f(Gate)                    (conduction, stateless)
   I_out    = Ku*I_pu + Kd*I_pd + clamps
   C_comp * dV/dt = I_out
   |
   v
ngspice subckt (B + C elements)                                  [Step E]
   |
   v
validate: long-pulse match, then short-pulse divergence          [Step F]
```

The rise/fall tables are consumed offline; at run time only the gate states evolve,
and conduction is read off them. Because gate charge is continuous, a mid-pulse
reversal is handled with no reset, no table switch, and no trajectory mismatch.
